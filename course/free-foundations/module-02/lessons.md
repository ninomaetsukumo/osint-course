# F02.01 — Turning a Topic into a Research Question

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Distinguish a topic from a research question, convert unclear requests into focused questions, and identify information needed to answer them.

## A topic is not a research question
"Research Northbridge Trading Ltd." is a topic, not a clear investigation question. A question such as "Was Northbridge Trading Ltd. recorded as an active registered company on 30 September 2026?" identifies a subject, issue and date. Northbridge is a fictional teaching example.

## What makes a useful question?
Identify the subject, issue, time period, and evidence that could reasonably resolve the question. Do not prejudge the answer. Rather than asking "Why is Northbridge secretly operating without approval?", ask "What public records, if any, establish Northbridge's approval and operating status?"

## Break complex questions apart
"Was the new branch approved, opened, and commercially operating by 30 September?" contains three questions: Was it officially approved? Did it physically open? Did commercial activity begin before the deadline? Each may require different evidence.

## Guided activity
Rewrite the broad topic "Investigate the new community center" as a focused question. Example: "What authorized public records show whether the new community center opened by 1 October?"

## Lesson check — five questions (pass 4/5)
1. A topic and a research question are the same thing.
2. A useful research question should identify the issue being investigated.
3. A research question may contain several separate claims that need checking.
4. A researcher should assume an allegation is true when writing the question.
5. A relevant date can help make a research question more precise.

# F02.02 — Defining Research Scope and Boundaries

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Define research scope; identify relevant and irrelevant information; establish legal, ethical and time boundaries; recognize when authorization is needed.

## What is research scope?
Research scope defines what is included or excluded. For an investigation into whether a fictional library was approved before 30 September, the relevant scope includes official approval records and announcements from the applicable period. Unrelated details about employees or their families and restricted accounts are outside scope.

## Four important boundaries
**Subject:** Which organization, location or event? **Time:** Which dates matter? **Sources:** Which lawful, authorized sources may be used? **Purpose:** What information is necessary to answer the question?

## Research limitations
Not every question can be answered using public information. Some documents are unavailable or incomplete. Record gaps rather than guessing.

## Why boundaries matter
Information appearing publicly does not eliminate privacy concerns. Do not use borrowed passwords or access restricted accounts. Detailed privacy and operational security are covered in Module 05.

## Guided activity
A client asks whether a fictional training provider was authorized to offer a course. In scope: an official authorization, its effective date and its stated limitations. Out of scope: a teacher's unrelated home address.

## Lesson check — five questions (pass 4/5)
1. What does research scope control?
2. Can a time boundary make an investigation more focused?
3. Does public availability eliminate all privacy concerns?
4. Should unavailable information be identified as a limitation?
5. Can a research question justify using an unauthorized employee account?

# F02.03 — Preparing a Research Plan

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Create a simple research plan, organize research tasks, identify appropriate sources, and set reasonable stopping criteria.

## Why plan before searching?
A plan saves time by defining what must be established before collection. Beginners can use a text document or spreadsheet; specialist software is not required.

## Five-part beginner plan
1. **Research question:** What must be established?
2. **Scope:** Which dates, events, records and boundaries apply?
3. **Sources:** Which lawful, relevant sources should be checked?
4. **Evidence requirements:** What records could answer the question?
5. **Stopping criteria:** When can the researcher reasonably report the findings?

## Example plan
**Question:** Did a fictional museum receive permission to open by 1 September?
**Scope:** Official authorization records and relevant public announcements before the deadline.
**Possible sources:** Municipal records and official notices.
**Evidence needed:** A formal decision and relevant effective date.
**Stopping point:** Report whether records establish approval and state any unresolved issues.

## Plans can change
A plan does not guarantee that expected sources exist. When evidence reveals a gap or changes the investigation, document an appropriate change to the plan.

## Guided activity
Write a five-part plan to investigate whether a fictional school obtained public authorization for a new training program.

## Lesson check — five questions (pass 4/5)
1. Should research planning happen before collecting unrelated information?
2. Is a research plan a guaranteed conclusion?
3. Should likely evidence sources be identified during planning?
4. Can the plan be revised when new information appears?
5. Should a research plan include stopping criteria?

# F02.04 — Understanding Sources and Evidence

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Distinguish sources and evidence, identify primary and secondary sources in context, understand limitations, and avoid confusing repetition with independent corroboration.

## What is a source?
A source is the origin of information, such as an official registry, statement, news article or published study. A source's existence does not prove that all statements within it are accurate.

## What is evidence?
Evidence is information used to support or challenge a particular claim. A municipal final decision can be evidence of authorization, but not proof that the approved facility subsequently opened. A genuine source can still be insufficient for a given question.

## Primary and secondary sources
A **primary source** is an original record or direct statement relevant to the matter, such as a municipal decision. A **secondary source** reports on or interprets other material, such as a newspaper article describing the decision. Primary does not mean infallible, and secondary does not mean automatically unreliable.

## Independent corroboration
Three websites repeating the same unsupported original article do not necessarily provide three independent confirmations. Trace repeated claims to their origins. Module 04 examines corroboration in detail.

## Claim, reason and evidence
Ask: **Claim:** What am I saying? **Reason:** Why do I think it? **Evidence:** Which identifiable record supports that reason? *The Craft of Research* distinguishes claims, reasons and supporting evidence.

## Guided activity
A fictional newspaper announces that a branch is approved but cites only a planning committee recommendation. Look for a final authorization record, if publicly available, before treating approval as established.

## Lesson check — five questions (pass 4/5)
1. Is a source the same as a conclusion?
2. Can an original official document still have limitations?
3. Do three articles relying on one report necessarily provide three independent confirmations?
4. Should claims be connected to identifiable supporting evidence?
5. Does approval automatically prove an activity actually started?

# F02.05 — Building an Evidence Log

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Describe the purpose of an evidence log, record source details, distinguish event from publication dates, and keep a traceable research record.

## What is an evidence log?
An evidence log records where information came from, what it states, when it was accessed, and why it matters. It supports a reproducible research process.

## Evidence-log fields
| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Evidence ID | Unique label such as E001 |
| Source title | Name of the record or publication |
| Issuer or author | Who produced it |
| Source location | Original URL or record reference |
| Publication date | When it was published |
| Event date | When the described event happened, if known |
| Access date | When it was consulted |
| Relevant information | What it actually states |
| Limitations | What it does not establish |

## Why dates matter
A record published on 3 October may describe a decision made on 29 September. Event and publication dates are different. Both matter when the research question concerns a deadline.

## Record facts, not assumptions
Avoid the log entry "E001 — Library opened" when the source only confirms authorization. Write "E001 — Official approval recorded; opening not established by this source."

## Guided activity
Make one evidence-log record containing its ID, title, issuing body, source location, publication date, event date if known, access date, relevant statement and limitations. Use a fictional record if practicing offline.

## Lesson check — five questions (pass 4/5)
1. Why use an evidence ID?
2. Should the source location be recorded?
3. Are event and publication dates necessarily identical?
4. Should the evidence log include known limitations?
5. Is an evidence log a substitute for evaluating sources?
