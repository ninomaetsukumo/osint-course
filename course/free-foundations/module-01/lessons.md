# F01.01 — What Is Open-Source Intelligence?
**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Explain what OSINT means, distinguish information from intelligence, and understand why publicly available information must still be evaluated.

## Understanding OSINT
**OSINT stands for Open-Source Intelligence.** It is a research process that uses publicly available or lawfully accessible information to answer a defined question.

Potential open sources include government publications, company websites, public registries, news reports, research papers, and publicly accessible online records.

OSINT is not simply collecting information from the internet. The important steps are choosing relevant sources, checking what they actually establish, and producing a finding that someone can use.

## Information versus intelligence
Imagine a company's website says: "We plan to open a new branch next month."

That statement is information. It tells us what the company publicly announced.

Suppose your research question is whether the branch is already operating. The announcement does not answer that question. You would need suitable evidence of actual operation before reaching that conclusion.

Intelligence is the evaluated result of research performed to answer a question. A good OSINT finding identifies both what is known and what remains uncertain.

## Public does not always mean appropriate
Finding information online does not automatically mean you should collect, retain, or publish it.

A responsible researcher considers the purpose of the investigation, applicable laws, authorization, privacy, and risks to others. Students work with lawful public information and fictional practice cases. No intrusive access or advanced software is required.

## Guided example
**Research question:** Has a new public library opened?

- A council announcement says construction has been approved.
- A news article says the library will open next month.
- No evidence of an actual opening has been provided.

**Appropriate finding:** The available sources establish an approved project and a planned opening, not that the library has already opened.

## Practice activity
Write one sentence for each:
1. A public information source you could use in a business investigation.
2. A question that source might help answer.
3. Something that source alone might not establish.

## Lesson check (5 questions; pass 4/5)
1. What does OSINT stand for?
2. What is the central purpose of OSINT research?
3. A company announces a future opening. What is established?
4. Does publicly accessible information always justify publication?
5. Which is a good beginner OSINT practice?

# F01.02 — Where OSINT Is Used
**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Explain common uses of OSINT, identify suitable public sources for different questions, and recognize that the same research method applies across multiple fields.

## Common applications
| Field | Example research question | Possible public sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Journalism | When did an organization announce a policy? | Official statements, archived publications |
| Business research | Is a company legally registered? | Relevant official company registry |
| Due diligence | Which public facts support a business claim? | Filings, official notices |
| Cybersecurity | What information has an organization intentionally made public about its systems? | Authorized public documentation |
| Human rights investigations | What can public digital materials establish about an event? | Public imagery, publications, records |
| Academic research | What published evidence addresses a research question? | Studies and institutional publications |

Different projects have different legal and ethical requirements. OSINT methods do not grant additional permission to access restricted systems or private accounts.

## Match the source to the question
Suppose the question is whether a company is registered. An official corporate registry may be relevant.

If the question is whether that company has started serving customers at a new branch, company registration is insufficient. A researcher must look for evidence relating to that specific activity.

**Remember:** A source can be authentic and still be insufficient for the question.

## A tool is not a method
You do not need advanced software to begin OSINT. A web browser, a clear research question, a simple note-taking document, and careful reasoning are enough for introductory exercises. Tools can increase efficiency later, but they cannot turn weak evidence into strong evidence.

## Guided exercise
A client asks whether a training institution offers a particular certificate.

Start with the institution's official course description and the relevant credentialing authority, where applicable. Record the difference between a course advertising a certificate and a credential being recognized by another organization.

## Lesson check (5 questions; pass 4/5)
1. Is an official registry generally more directly relevant to company-registration status than an unrelated social-media comment?
2. Can an official company website establish what that company announced?
3. Does registration alone establish actual trading at a branch?
4. Are expensive OSINT tools required for introductory research?
5. Should the researcher choose sources based on the research question?

# F01.03 — The Intelligence Cycle
**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Identify the stages of the intelligence cycle, explain what each stage does, and apply the process to a simple investigation.

## Why a structured process matters
Beginning a search without understanding the question often leads to irrelevant results.

The intelligence cycle offers a repeatable process, helping researchers organize their work from the initial request to the final report and feedback.

Rae Baker's *Deep Dive*, Chapter 2, discusses a six-stage version incorporating feedback:

1. **Planning and requirements:** What do we need to know?
2. **Collection:** Find relevant, authorized information.
3. **Processing and evaluation:** Organize and assess what was found.
4. **Analysis and production:** Develop supported findings.
5. **Dissemination and consumption:** Present findings to their intended user.
6. **Feedback:** Clarify needs and identify further questions.

Feedback may lead to revised questions and another research cycle.

## Walkthrough
**Question:** Has a fictional community center opened?

- **Planning:** Define what counts as opening and the date being checked.
- **Collection:** Examine authorized public notices and official announcements.
- **Processing and evaluation:** Record source details, publication dates, and what each source states.
- **Analysis:** Separate approval, planned opening, and confirmed activity.
- **Dissemination:** Write a short conclusion with evidence and limitations.
- **Feedback:** Ask whether the client needs another date checked or additional verification.

## Common beginner mistake
Researchers sometimes begin with collection because searching feels productive. But a large collection of unrelated results is not the same as an answer. Planning first helps avoid unnecessary work.

## Lesson check (5 questions; pass 4/5)
1. Which phase comes first?
2. In which phase are relevant sources gathered?
3. Which phase turns evaluated information into findings?
4. Is dissemination simply collecting more web pages?
5. Can feedback lead to another cycle?

# F01.04 — Facts, Assumptions, and Conclusions
**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Distinguish documented facts, assumptions, analytical conclusions, and unknowns; recognize unsupported reasoning; and write appropriately qualified findings.

## Four different categories
| Category | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Documented fact | Something supported by an identified record | A council register records approval dated 18 September |
| Assumption | Something accepted provisionally without adequate verification | Approval means the branch must already be open |
| Analytical conclusion | A reasoned judgment drawn from evidence | Formal approval is established, but opening is not |
| Unknown | Something the available evidence cannot resolve | Whether customers were served before 30 September |

A documented record proves what it records within its scope. It does not necessarily prove every related real-world event.

## The danger of assumptions
Heuer's *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis* explains how mental models and assumptions influence how analysts interpret evidence. Analysts should actively challenge their interpretations and consider alternatives.

Ask:
- What exactly does the source establish?
- Am I adding something that the source does not say?
- Could another explanation fit the available evidence?
- What evidence might change my conclusion?

## Use careful language
Avoid: "The branch definitely opened because it was approved."

Instead: "The available record confirms approval. It does not establish that the branch opened."

The second statement separates known information from an unresolved question.

## Practice activity
Classify:
- An official document records permission to construct a building.
- The building must therefore be occupied.
- The supplied sources do not establish occupancy.
- The actual number of people using the building is not documented.

Possible categories: documented fact about permission; assumption; evidence-limited conclusion; unknown.

## Lesson check (5 questions; pass 4/5)
1. Does an assumption automatically become a fact through repetition?
2. Can an authentic document have limited evidentiary scope?
3. Should an analyst consider plausible alternative explanations?
4. Is an evidence-limited conclusion preferable to unsupported certainty?
5. Should important unknowns be reported?

# F01.05 — Thinking Like an OSINT Researcher
**Estimated study time:** 15 minutes

## Learning objectives
Practice disciplined questioning, avoid premature conclusions, recognize research boundaries, and identify when available evidence is sufficient to report.

## Adopt a research mindset
An OSINT researcher should not begin with a conclusion and search only for supporting examples. Begin with a question, identify what evidence is needed, and evaluate relevant information—even when it contradicts the expected result.

## Five working habits
1. **Question clearly.** Define what the investigation must answer.
2. **Work methodically.** Follow a repeatable process instead of collecting unrelated information.
3. **Separate evidence from interpretation.** Explain why records support a conclusion.
4. **Challenge assumptions.** Consider alternative explanations and missing information.
5. **Know when to stop.** Report what is supported and unresolved rather than extending an investigation unnecessarily or crossing legal and ethical boundaries.

## A simple research checklist
- [ ] I can state the research question in one sentence.
- [ ] I know which public sources could be relevant.
- [ ] I can distinguish documented facts from assumptions.
- [ ] I can explain what remains unknown.
- [ ] I will stay within lawful, authorized research boundaries.

## Final lesson exercise
A fictional organization announces a service launch. A public regulator lists its authorization. A third-party article claims customers are already using the service but gives no independent evidence.

Write a short finding answering: **What can be established, and what remains unknown?**

A suitable answer distinguishes the organization's announcement, the recorded authorization, and the unverified assertion of actual service activity.

## Lesson check (5 questions; pass 4/5)
1. Should research begin with a predetermined conclusion?
2. Is recording uncertainty part of professional research?
3. Can multiple reports repeat the same unsupported original claim?
4. Is more searching always necessary?
5. Should conclusions be tied to identifiable evidence?
